# Module  1 Design Journal
Stephen Matthews | CECS-101: Design Your Degree, Design Your Career Module 1: Introduction to Design Thinking

Where I'm Starting

I'm 35 years old and I was out of school for about ten years before coming back. I grew up on a farm, served in the Marine Corps, danced professionally, and now run my own business while starting a degree in Applied AI. That's the context I'm bringing into this course, and it means some of the Module 1 material fits me differently than it fits a traditional first-year student.

Design Thinking and Wicked Problems

The idea that stuck with me first was the wicked problem. These are problems that are complex, shifting, and personal, with no single right answer and conditions that change while you're working on them. Choosing a career is one of those. The linear approach I've been trained in twice over, where you identify the problem, pick the best option, and execute, works well when the problem holds still. It doesn't hold still here.

What's interesting is that I've already practiced both approaches without having names for them. The Marine Corps runs on linear problem-solving. You assess, decide, execute, and don't relitigate the decision. Dance ran on the opposite. You attempt something, someone tells you what's wrong, you adjust, and you attempt it again. Thousands of times. That's the iterative loop this chapter describes, and I spent years inside it without ever calling it design thinking.

The chapter's third example says that any time you've solved a real problem by paying attention to what was actually happening instead of what you assumed was happening, you were already doing a version of this. That reframes the course for me. I'm not learning a foreign method. I'm learning to apply a familiar one somewhere I've never pointed it.

The Phase I Expect to Find Hardest

I expect Ideate to be the hardest phase for me, and for the opposite reason the chapter assumes.

The material is written for someone who doesn't know what they want. That's the common first-year condition, and it isn't mine. I know precisely what I want to build, which is an application that teaches dance at home using computer vision to correct technique and timing. I've had that idea for years. Coming into this course I'm not short on direction. I'm short on foundations.

Ideate asks you to generate possibilities broadly and boldly, without filtering, before narrowing. I'll want to skip it. I already have my answer, so every alternative will feel like a detour from work I could be doing instead. But the whole premise of the phase is that the first answer isn't automatically the best one, and I have no way to know whether mine is, because I've never seriously considered another. I arrived at it through my own history rather than through exploration.

I've noticed something similar about the process overall. Design thinking runs Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test. I've effectively started at Prototype. That's a real risk, and naming it now is more useful than discovering it in Module 4.

The Myth of Having It Figured Out

The chapter says most students don't know what they want, and that uncertainty is the appropriate condition for someone just beginning. My situation is the inverse, and I think it carries its own version of the same danger.

The student performing certainty and the student with genuine conviction can look identical from the outside, and from the inside too. A specific goal is an advantage right up until it becomes a reason to stop asking questions. The line I keep returning to is that the goal is not certainty, the goal is a better question. For someone in my position, that means holding the dance app loosely enough to keep testing it, rather than treating it as settled and spending four years defending a decision I made before I knew anything about the field.

Growth Mindset

Dweck's framework identified two things I say about myself that are textbook fixed mindset.

The first is that I'm not a good writer. I've stated that as a fact about my nature rather than as a description of a skill I haven't practiced. The fixed-mindset column in the chapter's table starts with exactly that sentence, and the growth version adds one word, which is "yet." It's a small change that I don't fully believe yet, and that's probably the point.

The second is bigger. My most persistent worry about this degree is that I won't be able to catch up, that the field moves too fast and I started too late without a computer science background. Reading the mindset material, I recognize that as a claim about fixed ability rather than about effort or time. It's also contradicted by my own record. I've started from zero in four unrelated fields and become competent in every one. The fear was identical each time and wrong each time. This is the fifth instance of a pattern with a consistent outcome.

What I take from Dweck is that the mindset itself isn't fixed either. Noticing my default is the first move.

The Transition

Most of the high school to college comparison doesn't describe me. I've built my own schedule, managed my own deadlines, and answered to no supervisor for years, because running a business requires all of it. The "what changes" column is mostly things I already do.

The row that does apply is community. My social circle isn't assigned, and it isn't going to assemble itself. I've learned nearly everything alone and that's worked, but the chapter's point about the emotional adjustment being harder than the academic one is worth taking seriously, especially returning after a decade away, in classrooms where I'll be the oldest person by a wide margin.

The other line that lands is that the habits you built come with you, good and bad. Mine include real discipline and a tendency toward intensity that people comment on consistently. Both are arriving with me. Only one of them is entirely an asset.

Resources I Plan to Use

Naming these matters more than knowing they exist, since my instinct is to absorb difficulty quietly rather than ask.

CECS Advising. My degree is interdisciplinary by design, and I want help sequencing it since I'm starting without prerequisites.
Tutoring and academic support. From week three, not week ten. Math is where I'm furthest behind and where errors compound silently.
The Writing Center. Three drafts this semester, aimed directly at the fixed-mindset belief above.
Veteran support services. There are benefits and resources available to me that I haven't fully used. Using something that exists isn't weakness.
Career Services. The chapter says to go earlier than you think, and I have a specific enough target that a first-year conversation would be useful rather than premature.
What I Want This Portfolio to Show

Not that I had it right at the start. I'd rather it show my thinking actually changing, including where a first answer got tested and replaced, and what the evidence was. If the dance app survives four years of this process, I want the record to show that it survived scrutiny rather than avoided it. And if it doesn't survive, I want to be able to see exactly where it broke, and what I built instead.
